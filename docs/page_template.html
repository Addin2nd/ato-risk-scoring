Case study · Fraud & security data science

Catching account takeovers at the moment of login

A risk engine that scores every login from 0 to 100 and decides on the spot: let the user in, ask for MFA, or block the session and alert the SOC. It combines detection rules, anomaly detection and a supervised model, and its thresholds come from a cost model that states the business's risk tolerance.

Synthetic data{{events}} simulated login events from {{users}} users over four months, with injected credential-stuffing, phishing and brute-force campaigns. The figures describe this simulation, not a production system.

The problem

The password is correct, so the login looks normal

In an account takeover the attacker already has the victim's real credentials, from phishing, a breach dump replayed at scale (credential stuffing), or guessing. Password checks pass. What gives the attacker away is behaviour: a device the account has never used, a datacenter network, a jump from Surabaya to Moscow in two hours, a burst of failed attempts across many accounts.

The difficult part is the legitimate traffic that looks just as strange: people on holiday, privacy-VPN users, new phones, and thousands of subscribers sharing a single mobile-carrier IP. A useful engine has to tell these apart, explain every decision to an analyst, and give auditors evidence that the control works.

Approach

From raw logs to a decision in five steps

  1. Point-in-time features

    27 signals per login, each computed only from earlier events: new device, country or ASN for this user, travel speed, hour deviation, failed-login pressure per account, IP and ASN. A unit test proves future data never leaks into a past feature.

  2. Three detectors

    Nine transparent rules for known patterns, an Isolation Forest that needs no labels, and gradient boosting trained on confirmed takeovers.

  3. One calibrated score

    A logistic stacker, fitted on out-of-fold predictions in time order, blends the three into a probability. That probability is mapped to a log-scale 0–100 score.

  4. Thresholds from cost

    Grid search for the MFA and block cut-offs that minimise expected fraud loss + customer friction + analyst time, with a cap on how many real users get challenged.

  5. Alerts & evidence

    HIGH decisions go to the SIEM as ECS JSON with reason codes. A separate detector rate-limits IPs that spray many accounts. KPIs are recomputed on every run as audit evidence.

Findings

What I learned

  • {{r01_prec}}%

    Impossible-travel rules are mostly noise

    The classic rule fires about {{r01_day}} times a day at {{r01_prec}}% precision. VPN users and travellers switching between roaming and hotel Wi-Fi trip it constantly. "Odd login hour" is worse: about {{r09_day}} times a day at {{r09_prec}}% precision.

  • {{rob_hybrid}}%

    The hybrid earns its place when labels are missing

    On known attacks it ties the supervised model. When credential-stuffing labels were hidden from training, recall on that attack was {{rob_gbm}}% for gradient boosting alone and {{rob_hybrid}}% for the hybrid; the label-free Isolation Forest reached {{rob_iso}}%. The sample is small (n = {{rob_n}}), so I treat this as a direction, not a result.

  • 0

    Never hard-block a mobile carrier IP

    Attackers route through residential proxies and CGNAT addresses shared with real customers. Blocking only hosting IPs for 24 hours, and rate-limiting the rest, hit {{ip_hit}} of {{ip_total}} attack attempts and hard-blocked {{ip_legit_blocked}} real logins.

  • 10×

    A log-scale score is easier to explain

    "Every 25 points is ten times more likely" is something a fraud manager or auditor can reason with. A raw probability of 0.0003 is not.

Limitations

What I would check before trusting these numbers

  • Simulated attackers are simpler than real ones and do not adapt to the controls.
  • The test window has only {{test_ato}} takeovers, so the confidence intervals are wide.
  • Labels here are perfect and immediate. Real labels arrive late and incomplete, through customer reports and chargebacks.
  • Next steps: run on the public RBA dataset, add device-reputation and graph features, and set up weekly drift monitoring with threshold recalibration.
